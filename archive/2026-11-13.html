From East Africa's rainy season to new diplomatic efforts in Europe.

KENYA · WEATHER · FLOOD PREPARATION

Kenya prepares for heavy El Niño rains as families remember past floods

WHAT HAPPENED

Kenya is preparing for unusually heavy rain during the October-to-December rainy season. The Kenya Meteorological Department expects November to be an especially wet month in many areas. The World Meteorological Organization says the wider Greater Horn of Africa is likely to be warmer and wetter than usual as El Niño strengthens. The Associated Press reported on November 13 that officials warn up to 1.5 million people in Kenya could be affected by flooding.

WHY IT MATTERS

Kenya is in East Africa, beside the Indian Ocean. Its seasonal rains refill rivers, water supplies and grazing land. But too much rain in a short time can overflow rivers, damage roads and homes, interrupt school and make drinking water unsafe. The warning is a risk estimate—not a prediction that every person in the 1.5 million will be flooded.

THE HARD PART

In Nairobi, some homes are built close to rivers because safer housing can be too expensive or far from work. A World Resources Institute study says flood danger is made worse by blocked drains, lost wetlands, weak land-use planning and poor infrastructure—not rainfall alone. Moving away from a dangerous riverbank can protect lives, but families also need somewhere safe and affordable to go.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

County officials are clearing drains and rubbish from waterways, preparing 172 shelters with food and medicine, and planning phone alerts with Safaricom. Community groups are helping clean river channels and share warnings. Weather forecasts give people and emergency teams time to prepare before the heaviest rain arrives.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

More rain can help farmers and livestock after dry periods, but sudden floods can destroy crops and homes. Moving families away from high-risk places may reduce danger, yet relocation must respect people's needs for jobs, school and community. Long-term drainage, restored wetlands and safe housing cost money, but can prevent much greater losses later.

UKRAINE · EUROPE · DIPLOMACY

New Ukraine peace talks may include European countries—but major disagreements remain

WHAT HAPPENED

U.S. envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Russian President Vladimir Putin in Moscow on November 12, then travelled to Dublin to meet Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. On Friday, November 13, Reuters reported that Kushner said Russia and Ukraine had agreed to include European countries in the next round of talks. The U.S. is also working toward a large prisoner exchange that could reunite thousands of people with their families, but no number or date has been announced.

WHY IT MATTERS

Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine began in February 2022. The war has separated families, damaged homes and energy systems, and forced millions of people to leave their homes. A real ceasefire and lasting peace could make life safer for people in Ukraine and help reduce wider tensions in Europe. A prisoner exchange could bring some people home even before a full agreement is reached.

THE HARD PART

Russia and Ukraine still disagree sharply about territory and security. Russia controls about one-fifth of Ukraine, while Ukraine refuses to give up the rest of its eastern Donbas region. Russia says NATO's military presence near its borders threatens its security. Ukraine says it needs strong guarantees so that another attack does not happen after a ceasefire. European countries also want a voice because any agreement would affect their security.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

U.S. envoys are carrying messages between Moscow and Kyiv. The next round is expected to include European representatives, although the exact format is not settled. Negotiators are discussing security guarantees, ways to reduce fighting and a possible prisoner exchange. The earlier talks in Abu Dhabi produced no major breakthrough, but did lead to an agreement in principle on exchanging prisoners.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Talks can create a path to fewer attacks and bring captured people back to their families. But a quick deal that does not make people feel safe could leave the risk of another war. The governments involved have different goals, and a meeting does not mean the fighting has stopped. A lasting agreement usually takes difficult decisions and trust built over time.

💰 Economy & Business

Oil prices are easing, but the journey from a barrel of crude to a price at the pump is complicated.

Illustrative crude-oil tanker on the St. Lawrence River near Quebec City, photographed in 2022. Photo: Wilfredor, CC0 public-domain dedication. Image details ↗
ENERGY · SHIPPING · PRICES

Oil prices fall again—but why might that not make everything cheaper right away?

WHAT HAPPENED

On Friday, November 13, Reuters reported that Brent crude—the main international oil-price benchmark—was about US$95.45 a barrel, while U.S. crude was about US$88.06. Both prices were lower that morning and were heading for a second weekly decline. The International Energy Agency said global oil supply rose by 2.3 million barrels a day in October to 105.4 million barrels a day, while it forecast average world demand of 102.3 million barrels a day for 2026.

WHY IT MATTERS

Oil is used to make gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, plastics and many chemicals. When crude oil becomes cheaper, it can eventually reduce costs for drivers, airlines, delivery companies and factories. A barrel is a standard oil-market unit equal to about 159 litres—but the price of crude is not the same as the price of gasoline at a local station.

THE HARD PART

Oil still has to be shipped, refined and delivered. Attacks on tankers and energy facilities around the Middle East keep shipping risks high, especially near the Strait of Hormuz, a narrow waterway used by many oil ships. The IEA says land-based oil stocks remain very low and diesel prices are still far above pre-war levels.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Oil-producing countries are restoring exports where it is safe to do so. Governments and energy agencies are watching supplies and keeping emergency reserves. Businesses are also trying to use energy more efficiently and, where possible, switch to electricity or other sources.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Lower oil prices can help families and businesses that buy fuel. But oil-producing regions may earn less, and a sudden price drop can make companies delay new projects. Prices can rise again if shipping is disrupted. Even when crude gets cheaper, local fuel prices may take time to change because refining costs, transport, taxes and exchange rates also matter.

🤖 Technology & Science

A future space telescope is learning to see the universe in a kind of light our eyes cannot see.

Artist's concept of NASA's UVEX spacecraft. Credit: SDL/NASA/JPL-Caltech, via Caltech. Image and mission details ↗
NASA · SPACE TELESCOPES · ULTRAVIOLET LIGHT

NASA's UVEX telescope passes a major milestone on its way to mapping the invisible sky

WHAT HAPPENED

On October 27, NASA confirmed that its new UVEX space telescope had passed an important technical and program review. The agency committed to launching it by January 2031, with a total mission cost of US$590 million. UVEX is still being built; it has not launched yet.

WHY IT MATTERS

UVEX stands for UltraViolet EXplorer. It will map the whole sky in ultraviolet light, which human eyes cannot see. Ultraviolet light is the same kind of invisible energy from the Sun that can cause sunburn. By studying it, scientists can learn how stars and galaxies grow and change, and spot powerful events such as exploding stars and collisions between neutron stars.

THE HARD PART

Space telescopes need detectors that can measure very faint light while surviving launch, cold, heat and radiation. Ultraviolet light is especially tricky to detect. UVEX must also send huge amounts of information back to Earth so astronomers can study the signals.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Engineers at Caltech and NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory are adapting CMOS sensor technology—the kind used in billions of smartphone cameras—to detect ultraviolet light. UVEX will be able to turn quickly toward alerts about cosmic explosions and observe them within hours.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

A new telescope can reveal things older instruments miss and create data that scientists around the world can use. But a US$590 million mission is a major public investment, and building space hardware takes years. Engineers must test carefully because repairs are difficult once a telescope is in orbit.

🎨 Creativity & Inspiration

A young researcher spent four years testing whether a familiar food could help reduce plastic waste.

Illustrative plantains at a market in Ghana; these are not the materials used in Rye's experiment. Photo: Uprising Man, CC BY-SA 4.0. Image details and licence ↗
MONTANA · STUDENT INVENTOR · BIOPLASTIC

Thirteen-year-old Rye Rost discovers that plantains can make a stronger bioplastic

WHAT HAPPENED

Rye Lynn Rost, a 13-year-old eighth grader from Baker, Montana, won second place in the technology division of the 2026 Thermo Fisher Scientific Junior Innovators Challenge. She compared starch from bananas and plantains, then made small samples of bioplastic and tested their strength. Her results showed that plantains had more starch and made a stronger material in her experiment.

WHY IT MATTERS

Most everyday plastic is made from fossil fuels. Bioplastics are materials made partly or entirely from biological sources such as plants. Rye wanted to explore whether food-based ingredients could become a useful material instead of relying on fossil fuels.

THE HARD PART

A plastic alternative needs to be strong enough to do its job. If it falls apart too quickly in water or sunlight, it may not be useful. But if it lasts for years, it may still create litter. A material also needs to break down under the right conditions, and not every product labelled “bio” will disappear quickly in nature.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Rye spent about four years improving her experiment. She counted starch-making cells, extracted starch and compared the finished samples. Her project is a promising student experiment, not a mass-produced replacement for all plastic. More testing would be needed for packaging, safety, cost and how the material breaks down.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Plant-based materials may reduce the need for fossil fuels and give researchers new ways to use natural ingredients. But growing crops uses land and water, and some bioplastics need special composting conditions. A good solution must work in real life, not only in a school laboratory.

🐾 Nature & Planet

A bird virus has become a serious conservation problem for animals that gather in huge colonies.

Southern elephant seal at Moltke Harbour, South Georgia, photographed in 2011. Photo: Liam Quinn, CC BY-SA 2.0. Image details and licence ↗
SOUTH GEORGIA · WILDLIFE · BIRD FLU

Bird flu pushes southern elephant seals into a more dangerous category

WHAT HAPPENED

The International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) has moved the southern elephant seal from “Least Concern” to “Vulnerable” on its Red List. The highly infectious H5N1 bird-flu virus has spread from birds to mammals and affected four of the five major southern elephant seal populations. In some colonies, more than 90% of newborn pups have died. The Guardian reported that more than 53,000 female elephant seals had died on South Georgia by 2025, although exact totals are difficult to confirm.

WHY IT MATTERS

Southern elephant seals are among the largest seals on Earth. They breed in crowded groups on remote islands, including South Georgia in the South Atlantic Ocean. When many animals gather close together, a virus can spread quickly. Losing many pups and adult females can make it harder for the population to recover.

THE HARD PART

Scientists cannot count every animal in every remote colony. Some seals die at sea, and researchers may not find their bodies. That means the exact number of deaths is uncertain. The IUCN category describes a higher risk of extinction; it does not mean the species is about to disappear tomorrow.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Scientists are monitoring colonies, testing animals where possible and comparing new counts with older records. They are also studying which species and locations are most at risk. Careful monitoring helps people understand whether an outbreak is slowing down or spreading to new places.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Visiting colonies to collect information can help scientists, but getting too close may disturb animals or carry germs between groups. Remote cameras and careful sampling can reduce some risks, although they do not answer every question. Conservation teams must protect wildlife while also collecting enough evidence to make good decisions.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

How could scientists learn whether a wildlife disease is spreading without disturbing the animals they are trying to protect?

🧠 Three words to keep

Seasonal forecast

A science-based estimate of the weather pattern expected over the next few months. It is a guide, not a promise.

Benchmark

A standard price or measurement used for comparison. Brent crude is a benchmark for many oil deals.

Vulnerable

A conservation category meaning a species faces a high risk of extinction in the wild if threats continue.
