CARIBBEAN · CLIMATE · YOUNG PEOPLE

Caribbean island hosts a major meeting about trade, climate and jobs

WHAT HAPPENED

Leaders from 56 Commonwealth countries are gathering in Antigua and Barbuda from November 1 to 4. The meeting is in St John's, the capital. The Commonwealth is a voluntary group of independent countries, not one country with one government. Its members are home to about 2.7 billion people.

WHY IT MATTERS

Leaders are discussing trade, investment, jobs and climate change. The Caribbean faces powerful storms and rising seas. The Associated Press notes that 49 of the 56 member countries have an ocean coastline, so protecting communities and oceans is a shared concern.

THE HARD PART

Countries have different needs and different amounts of money. A small island may need storm shelters, while a large country may focus on factories or digital jobs. Agreeing on a plan is only the first step; funding it and making sure people benefit can be harder.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

A Commonwealth Youth Forum is bringing together 300 young delegates. Leaders are also discussing a fund to teach digital skills to about 10 million young adults and support for countries shifting from fossil fuels towards renewable energy. These are plans, not finished projects.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Good: Countries can share ideas, training and investment. Risk: Big promises do not automatically improve a student's school, a farmer's income or an island's safety. People will need to check whether the plans become useful projects and whether benefits are shared fairly.

If you were a young delegate, what would you ask leaders to solve first — safer coastlines, better schools, digital skills, jobs or something else?

NORTH AFRICA · DIPLOMACY · PEACEKEEPING

UN peacekeeping mission in Western Sahara begins winding down after 35 years

WHAT HAPPENED

The UN mission known as MINURSO began winding down on November 1 after the Security Council did not agree on a new mandate. Since 1991, it had monitored a ceasefire and helped prepare a public vote, called a referendum, about the territory's future. The vote never happened.

WHY IT MATTERS

Western Sahara is a mostly desert territory on the Atlantic coast of north-west Africa, south of Morocco and north of Mauritania. Morocco claims it and controls most of it. The Polisario Front seeks independence and is backed by Algeria. The UN says it still supports a peaceful solution that respects the people's right to decide their future.

THE HARD PART

The sides disagree about the territory's future and who could vote. The ceasefire broke down in 2020, and low-level hostilities have continued. Russia vetoed a US-backed renewal plan; a competing Russian proposal also failed. Morocco welcomed the closure, while the Polisario Front and Algeria criticized the deadlock.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

A smaller UN team will oversee the withdrawal of national troops and disposal of UN equipment. Secretary-General António Guterres says the UN remains ready to support a just, lasting and mutually acceptable political solution.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Change: The UN is changing how it uses peacekeeping resources. Concern: With fewer monitors on the ground, it may be harder for outside observers to follow events. Ending the mission does not settle the dispute or decide the territory's future.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

What would make a solution fair to the people who live there? Who should be included in the conversation?

💰 Economy & Business

ENERGY · SHIPPING · EVERYDAY PRICES

Oil prices fall on hopes for diplomacy — but the shipping problem is not solved

WHAT HAPPENED

On Monday, November 2, Brent crude oil fell about 3.9% to US$109.01 a barrel in early trading. It was still much higher than the roughly US$72 level in late February, before the war. Prices moved down after fresh hopes for diplomatic progress between the United States and Iran.

WHY IT MATTERS

The Strait of Hormuz, between Iran and Oman, is an important route for oil and gas ships. If ships cannot pass safely, fewer supplies may reach buyers. Oil powers cars, trucks, ships, planes and factories, so supply problems can affect transport and the price of some goods.

THE HARD PART

Iran's foreign minister said negotiators in Qatar were discussing a proposal that could reopen the strait within two days if the US met several conditions. He also said there were no direct talks with the US. A proposal is not an agreement, and market prices can change quickly.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Diplomats are exploring ways to reduce the danger and reopen shipping. Governments, energy buyers and shipping companies are watching the route and adjusting plans.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Good: Cheaper oil and safe shipping could ease fuel and transport costs. Bad: Price swings make planning difficult. A market-price drop also does not instantly change gas-station prices; refining, taxes and timing matter too.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

Choose one thing you used today — a snack, pencil, phone or pair of shoes. How many journeys might it have taken by truck, ship or plane before it reached you?

SPACE · ASTRONOMY · SATELLITES

After 22 years of discoveries, NASA's Swift telescope is preparing to come home

WHAT HAPPENED

NASA ended science operations of the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory after its orbit dropped too low for the telescope to point steadily. Its final observation was on October 20. The US Space Force predicted it could re-enter Earth's atmosphere around Friday, November 6, but the exact time and location are uncertain.

WHY IT MATTERS

Swift launched in November 2004 for a two-year mission and worked for almost 22. It detected more than 2,000 gamma-ray bursts — powerful flashes linked to events such as star deaths or collisions — and studied about 1,400 supernovas, or exploding stars. Its quick alerts helped other telescopes turn towards these events.

THE HARD PART

A private rescue spacecraft called LINK tried to push Swift into a higher orbit, but control problems stopped it from reaching the telescope. Most of Swift is expected to burn up during re-entry, though a few dense pieces might survive. Any surviving pieces are most likely to fall into the ocean.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

NASA is preserving Swift's public archive of nearly 22 years of observations — about 12 terabytes of data. Scientists will keep studying it while NASA looks for other ways to react quickly to cosmic explosions.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Good: Swift helped produce about 7,000 scientific papers. Hard part: Satellites do not stay in orbit forever. Designing spacecraft that can be repaired or moved to a safer orbit costs money and requires new technology.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

If you designed the next Swift telescope, what would you add so it could be repaired, refuelled or moved to a safer orbit?

AI IN BUSINESS · ABU DHABI

AI is moving into the energy business

On November 2, Abu Dhabi's energy company ADNOC and AI company G42 signed an agreement with OpenAI to use advanced AI across ADNOC's operations. The companies have not yet listed every task or shown results. OpenAI's Sam Altman also pointed to energy as a major challenge for AI: powerful computer systems need chips, data centres and electricity. The test will be whether the benefits justify the cost and energy use.

YOUNG INVENTOR · HEALTH · ENVIRONMENT

A teenager builds a low-cost tool to collect clues from the air

WHAT HAPPENED

Bailey Quinn Frank, 15, from Florida, won the top prize at the 2026 Thermo Fisher Scientific Junior Innovators Challenge, announced October 27. She built a low-cost environmental DNA sampler using a small fan and filter paper to collect airborne bacteria.

WHY IT MATTERS

Bailey collected two indoor air samples and one outdoor sample. Indoor samples had more human-associated bacteria; outdoor samples included bacteria associated with soil, water, air and plants. She hopes a simple tool could help rural communities collect information about possible health threats.

THE HARD PART

Collecting a sample is only the beginning. Scientists must identify what is in it and decide whether it matters. Bailey's project is a student prototype, not a medical test or a device that can diagnose disease. More testing with many more samples would be needed.

WHAT SHE IS TRYING

She used inexpensive materials, grew bacteria from some samples and sent others for DNA sequencing. The next challenge is to test the method carefully in different places.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Good: A simple design could make research tools less expensive. Limit: Cheap does not automatically mean accurate. People must not mistake an early clue for a confirmed health warning.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

What small problem at your school or in your neighbourhood could you investigate with a simple sensor? What would you measure, and how would you check your results?

CLIMATE SCIENCE · GLACIERS · OCEANS

New report warns that some ice losses could last for centuries

WHAT HAPPENED

A report released November 2 by the International Cryosphere Climate Initiative and the UN Environment Programme brings together more than 80 scientists. It warns that going above 1.5°C of warming could cause lasting damage to glaciers, polar ice sheets, sea ice and frozen ground. Earth has warmed about 1.3°C compared with pre-industrial times.

WHY IT MATTERS

The cryosphere is all Earth's frozen water: snow, glaciers, ice sheets, sea ice and permanently frozen ground. Ice helps keep sea levels lower, and mountain glaciers store water that millions of people depend on. Changes in frozen places can affect communities far away.

THE HARD PART

The report says Greenland and Antarctica's ice sheets are melting about six times faster than in the 1990s. Some losses would continue even if temperatures later fell back to 1.5°C. Many glaciers and ice systems take centuries or thousands of years to recover.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

The scientists urge countries to cut greenhouse-gas pollution quickly, expand renewable energy and batteries, and help communities prepare for rising seas and changing water supplies. The report says emissions need to fall by at least 5% each year, starting now, with net-zero emissions by mid-century.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Good: Every fraction of a degree avoided can limit damage. Some glaciers and sea ice could begin to recover if temperatures fall. Hard truth: There is no quick reset button, and some changes will last for generations.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

If your town wanted to prepare for hotter summers or heavier rain, what would you change first — more trees, better drainage, cooler buildings or something else?
