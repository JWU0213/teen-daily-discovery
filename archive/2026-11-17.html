Global News & Events

Two places, two difficult questions

East Asia · Airspace and diplomacy

Japan protests after two suspected Russian aircraft enter its airspace

Okinoshima is a small island off Fukuoka Prefecture in southwestern Japan, near the Tsushima Strait between Japan and the Korean Peninsula. On Tuesday morning, Japan said two aircraft it believed were Russian briefly entered the airspace above the area.

What happened

Japan's Ministry of Defense said one suspected Russian aircraft entered the area from about 4:07 to 4:10 a.m. local time, and a second from about 4:09 to 4:12 a.m. The two short entries overlapped, making the overall incident about five minutes long. Japan scrambled fighter jets and issued warnings. Tokyo then lodged a strong diplomatic protest with Moscow. The aircraft later crossed the Tsushima Strait and continued south.

Why it matters

Countries control the airspace above their land and territorial waters. A military aircraft entering without permission can create a serious safety and diplomatic problem. Pilots and radar teams must identify what is happening quickly, because a misunderstanding between military aircraft can become dangerous.

The hard part

Japan did not publicly identify the aircraft types or explain why they were there. The word “suspected” matters: Japan believes they were Russian, but the reports do not establish the pilots' reason. South Korea separately said Russian aircraft entered its air-defense identification zone on Monday, but stressed that they did not enter South Korean airspace. Those are two different things.

What people are trying

Japan used fighter jets to monitor the aircraft, issue warnings and show that the airspace was being watched. It also used diplomatic channels to ask Russia to prevent another incident. South Korea said it detected the aircraft and sent its own fighters to monitor them.

Good and bad / trade-offs

Monitoring aircraft can protect safety and help a country understand what is happening near its borders. But military responses can also raise tension. Clear communication, careful identification and diplomacy are important so that one brief flight does not lead to a much bigger problem.

Think about it

How could neighbouring countries protect their airspace while also reducing the chance of a dangerous misunderstanding?

Europe and East-Central Africa · Migration policy

Five European countries explore a new return centre in Rwanda

Rwanda is a landlocked country in the Great Lakes region of East-Central Africa. Its capital is Kigali. Five European Union countries — Austria, Denmark, Germany, Greece and the Netherlands — have announced that they are exploring a plan with Rwanda for a “return hub” outside Europe.

What happened

On November 16, the five countries and Rwanda said they would begin concrete talks about a return hub. The proposal is described as applying to adults who have no legal right to remain in the European country that made the decision, including people whose asylum claims have been rejected and whose appeals are finished. The exact rules, size and location are still being negotiated. No centre has opened and no final operating agreement has been signed.

Why it matters

People move between countries for many reasons: work, family, safety or a new start. An asylum seeker is someone asking a country for protection because they fear serious danger at home. Governments also have rules about who may stay. This proposal could change what happens to some people after their legal cases are finished.

The hard part

A decision must be accurate and fair. People need a real chance to explain why they fear returning home, access legal advice and use any appeal they are entitled to. A transfer must not put someone in danger. UNHCR, the UN Refugee Agency, has repeatedly warned that return hubs need strong safeguards and should only be used after a full and fair assessment of a person's protection claim.

What people are trying

The five governments say the hub could help carry out final return decisions and make it harder for people-smuggling networks to profit from dangerous journeys. Rwanda says people sent there could choose to return home voluntarily, build a life in Rwanda or move to another country where they have legal permission to live. UNHCR is giving advice on human-rights standards.

Good and bad / trade-offs

Supporters say a working return system can make migration rules more predictable and reduce the business of smugglers. Critics worry that sending people far from their families and support networks could make it harder to protect their rights. The result will depend on the final agreement, the safeguards and whether they work in real life.

Think about it

Before moving someone to another country, what protections would you want to see — for example, access to a lawyer, a fair appeal, safe housing or help to contact family?

Economy & Business

AI needs electricity — and someone has to pay for it

United States · Energy, business and household costs

New research measures the power used by America's biggest data centres

A data centre is a building filled with powerful computers called servers. They store photos, run websites, stream videos, host online games and help train or operate AI tools. The biggest facilities are sometimes called “hyperscale” data centres.

What happened

A Harvard-led study published on November 16 estimated electricity use at 403 of the largest U.S. data centres. For the year from May 2024 to April 2025, the researchers estimated they used between 68 and 99 terawatt-hours — about 1.8% of all U.S. electricity. The study estimated that 54% of their electricity came from fossil fuels and linked their power use to about 25.7 million metric tons of carbon dioxide.

Why it matters

When you ask an AI tool a question, save a photo online or stream a show, computers in data centres do work behind the scenes. They need electricity to run and often need cooling too. More demand can mean new power plants, transmission lines and cooling systems — all of which cost money. Those costs can affect businesses and, depending on local rules, electricity customers.

The hard part

The study covers only 403 very large U.S. facilities, not every data centre. Researchers estimated use from satellite images and operating scenarios; they did not have a meter reading for every computer. They also could not separate electricity used for AI from electricity used for other services. The measurement period ended in April 2025, so it is not a live total for today.

What people are trying

In the U.S. Mid-Atlantic and Midwest, grid operator PJM has proposed speeding up reviews for new power plants and large data centres. It wants to shorten the study process from about two years to 12 months and identify which upgrades are caused by new data centres, so companies could be charged more directly. The proposal still needs regulatory review. At COP31, the UN's International Telecommunication Union and Türkiye also launched a voluntary pledge encouraging cleaner power, efficient cooling, better water use and clearer reporting.

Good and bad / trade-offs

Data centres support useful services, research, jobs and new businesses. But if they use lots of fossil-fuel electricity, they add climate pollution; if they grow faster than the grid, local power costs and reliability can become concerns. Better efficiency, renewable electricity and reusing waste heat may help, but each solution takes planning and investment.

Think about it

If a company wanted to build a huge data centre near your town, what questions should residents ask about electricity, water, jobs, noise and who pays for new power lines?

Technology & Science

A star sends a radio signal every eight minutes

Astronomy · A rare pair of stars

Radio telescopes help solve part of a strange space mystery

What happened

Researchers at Australia's International Centre for Radio Astronomy Research (ICRAR) identified a rare pair of stars about 5,000 light-years away. One is a white dwarf — the dense leftover core of a star that has used up its fuel. The other is similar to our Sun. Radio signals from the system repeat roughly every 7.9 minutes. It is the first time astronomers have detected this kind of signal from a white dwarf orbiting a Sun-like star.

Why it matters

The discovery helps astronomers understand a puzzling group of objects called long-period transients. These objects switch radio signals on and off over minutes or hours. The signal also helped researchers notice powerful flares on the Sun-like companion star, a little like the activity that can help create auroras on Earth.

The hard part

The system is too far away to see with your eyes. Radio waves are invisible to humans, and the signals are faint. Scientists needed more than one kind of telescope and more than 100 hours of radio data to work out how the two stars move. They can identify where the pulses come from, but the exact physical process that makes them is still being studied.

What people are trying

The team first used the Murchison Widefield Array in Western Australia, then the MeerKAT radio telescope in South Africa to pinpoint the source. The W. M. Keck Observatory in Hawaiʻi helped confirm the Sun-like companion. Scientists hope future Square Kilometre Array telescopes in Australia and South Africa will find many more unusual systems.

Good and bad / trade-offs

Radio astronomy can reveal things ordinary cameras cannot see, and international telescope teams can combine their strengths. But building and running giant observatories takes years, money and careful teamwork. This discovery does not create a new gadget for your pocket; it gives scientists a better way to understand how stars behave.

Think about it

If you could build a telescope to detect something humans cannot see directly, what would you want to study — radio waves, infrared heat, X-rays or something else?

Creativity & Inspiration

Young people are building more than projects

Ontario, Canada · Teen-led game design

A Grade 11 student turns his love of coding into a game-making day for other teens

Dan Grishkov, a Grade 11 student at Newmarket High School in Ontario, organized a local event called Haven Newmarket for Sunday, November 15. It was designed to help teens aged 13–18 learn coding and make their own video games.

What happened

Grishkov planned a full day of beginner-friendly workshops using GitHub, Godot and Unity, followed by time for teams to create games and show them to one another. He has been coding since he was five and joined the nonprofit Hack Club about nine months ago. The public event page lists the schedule, but the sources checked for this edition do not yet confirm attendance numbers or winners.

Why it matters

A game jam is a short event where people make a game together, often around a theme or deadline. It teaches more than programming: participants practise planning, testing, explaining ideas, sharing jobs and solving problems when something does not work.

The hard part

Grishkov told NewmarketToday that finding a venue was difficult. He contacted about 25 possible locations, but only a handful replied. A local café, The Network Brew, eventually offered its space. Renting a venue would otherwise have cost more than $2,000.

What people are trying

The event was free and open to beginners, with workshops and mentors planned to help people get started. Hack Club supports teen-led coding projects and events around the world. The idea is to make technology feel like something young people can create, not just something they consume.

Good and bad / trade-offs

A free event lowers the cost barrier and gives beginners a friendly place to learn. But a one-day event cannot solve every problem: students still need access to computers, internet, time and support after the event ends. Community spaces and volunteer mentors can help make those opportunities last longer.

Think about it

If you organized a one-day game jam at your school, what theme would you choose? How could you make sure beginners felt welcome and every team had a chance to finish something?

Another community idea · Newmarket, Ontario

Students learn work skills one cup of coffee at a time

The Denison Brew Crew at Dr. J.M. Denison Secondary School is a student-run coffee service involving six students in grades 10–12. Started by teacher Julee Rose, it now includes customer service, shopping, community outings and practice using public transit. A local business owner covers supply costs, so money raised can help pay for transit passes and learning experiences. It is a reminder that a creative project does not have to be high-tech to help people build confidence and independence.

Nature & Planet

Mangrove forests are making a comeback — but not everywhere

Coasts around the world · A hopeful satellite discovery

NASA images show mangroves spreading along part of Myanmar's coast

Mangroves are trees and shrubs that grow where salty seawater meets the land. Their roots can look like stilts or tangled fingers. They create shelter for young fish and crabs, help hold shorelines together and can store large amounts of carbon.

What happened

A NASA Earth Observatory report published on November 13 describes a global study of mangrove forests using satellite images from 1984 to 2023. Researchers found losses of nearly 3,000 square kilometres between 1984 and 2010, followed by an increase of nearly 2,000 square kilometres after 2010. The overall direction shifted from loss toward recovery around 2010. In the Myanmar images above, the same coastal area looks greener in 2026 than it did in 2010.

Why it matters

Mangroves help protect coastal communities from erosion and storm surges. Their roots create nursery habitat for fish, shrimp and crabs, which support food and fishing livelihoods. NASA notes that mangrove ecosystems can store up to four times as much carbon as taller inland tropical forests.

The hard part

The recovery is uneven. Some areas in West and Central Africa, North America and southern Africa have continued to lose or damage mangrove forests. Myanmar lost a large share of its mangroves before 2010, and although its area has grown since then, deforestation remains a challenge. A global improvement does not mean every coastline is safe.

What people are trying

Researchers point to stronger forest protections, restoration projects and natural regrowth. About two-thirds of the expansion in the study came from mangroves spreading into new places; about one-third came from restoration and regrowth in damaged areas. Communities near Myanmar's coast have also experimented with conservation and tree-planting projects.

Good and bad / trade-offs

Protecting mangroves can help wildlife, store carbon and reduce storm damage. But coastal land is also used for farming, homes and aquaculture. Good restoration needs local people to help decide where trees can grow and how livelihoods will be protected. Planting new trees is useful, but protecting healthy forests already standing is important too.

Think about it

If your community lived beside a coast, how would you design a project that protects mangroves while also listening to fishers, farmers and families who depend on the land?

Vocabulary to Know

Three useful words from today's stories

Airspace

The part of the sky above a country or its territorial waters that the country controls.

Asylum seeker

A person asking another country for protection because they fear serious danger in their home country.

Hyperscale

Extremely large and powerful. A hyperscale data centre contains huge numbers of computers working together.

Book pick · Ages 8–12 THE MYSTERIOUS BENEDICT SOCIETY

The Mysterious Benedict Society by Trenton Lee Stewart

Four clever children answer a strange newspaper advertisement and pass a series of puzzles. They are invited on a secret mission at a school where something unusual is happening. To succeed, they must use observation, teamwork, courage and different kinds of intelligence.

Why read it? The story makes problem-solving feel like an adventure. It is a longer book — 512 pages — so it is a good choice if you enjoy mysteries, riddles and characters who solve problems together.
