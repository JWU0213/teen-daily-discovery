🌍 Global News & Events

Two very different challenges: protecting people during a disease outbreak, and getting countries to work together on climate solutions.

Illustrative historical photo from Ebola-response training in the United Kingdom in 2014; it is not a photo of the current outbreak. Photo: Simon Davis/DFID, CC BY 2.0. Image details and licence ↗
CENTRAL AFRICA · PUBLIC HEALTH · SCIENCE

Congo's Ebola outbreak passes 11,000 cases as vaccine research moves forward

WHAT HAPPENED

The Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), a large country in Central Africa, is facing its deadliest Ebola outbreak. Data reported for November 9 recorded 11,042 confirmed cases and 5,387 confirmed deaths in the DRC. The World Health Organization's regional table also lists cases in Uganda, Kenya, Nigeria and one imported case in France. The outbreak began in mid-May and has reached eight of the DRC's 26 provinces.

WHY IT MATTERS

Ebola is a serious disease that can spread through direct contact with the body fluids of someone who is sick or has died, or with objects contaminated by those fluids. It is not a reason to blame or avoid whole communities. People need accurate information, safe medical care and support for families. The outbreak is caused by the Bundibugyo type of Ebola virus, which is different from the type targeted by the best-known Ebola vaccine.

THE HARD PART

Health teams are working in places where armed conflict, poor roads and an overstretched health system make it difficult to reach patients. Misinformation and fear can also make people distrust medical workers. If clinics or health workers are attacked, it becomes even harder to test, treat and protect people.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Teams are tracing contacts, preparing treatment centres, supplying protective equipment and talking with local communities. On November 4, WHO recommended two vaccine candidates designed for the Bundibugyo virus for evaluation in an ongoing trial in the DRC. They still need national approvals before entering the trial. WHO says results from the vaccine study are expected in early 2027; treatment candidates are also being studied.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Research could give health workers more tools for future outbreaks, but a vaccine trial takes time and must be carefully checked for safety and effectiveness. A vaccine designed for one Ebola type does not automatically protect against every other type. WHO also stresses that vaccines alone will not end this outbreak: trust, safe clinics, trained staff and reliable information matter too.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

When people are frightened, rumours can spread quickly. What kind of message, in what language and from which trusted local voices, could help a community understand how to stay safe?

TÜRKIYE · CLIMATE · INTERNATIONAL COOPERATION

World leaders meet at COP31: promises now need practical plans

WHAT HAPPENED

The United Nations climate conference known as COP31 is taking place in Antalya, on Türkiye's Mediterranean coast, from November 9 to 20. Türkiye is hosting, while Australia is leading the formal negotiations. Nearly 200 countries are working on climate plans. On November 11, UN Secretary-General António Guterres warned that the world's response to climate change is moving too slowly.

WHY IT MATTERS

Climate change can affect the weather people experience, the food farmers grow, the water communities use and the safety of homes and roads. Countries are discussing how to use more electricity in transport, buildings and industry. Electric buses or heat pumps can reduce the use of fossil fuels, but the climate benefit depends partly on how that electricity is produced.

THE HARD PART

Countries have different needs and starting points. Some still need to expand reliable electricity access; others are trying to replace coal, oil and gas while keeping energy affordable. The conference host has proposed raising electricity's share of global energy use to 35% by 2035. Critics point out that using more electricity does not automatically mean using cleaner energy if the power still comes from fossil fuels.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Negotiators are discussing electricity, clean-energy investment and how communities can prepare for extreme weather. Countries and businesses are also announcing practical projects, such as rooftop solar and stronger power grids. The next question is whether these plans will receive funding, clear rules and workers who can build and maintain them.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Cleaner electricity can reduce pollution and make countries less dependent on imported fuels. But new power lines, batteries and renewable-energy projects cost money and take time to build. A fair plan also needs to consider workers and families who could face higher costs during the change. A target is useful only if people can see how it will be reached.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

If a country promises to use more electricity, what else should it promise: that the power is cleaner, that bills stay affordable, that supply is reliable, or all three? How could leaders measure progress?

💰 Economy & Business

A climate promise becomes real when households can afford the equipment, workers can install it and the electricity system can handle it.

Illustrative photo of rooftop solar panels; not a photograph of a home in one of the countries making the pledge. Photo: Esthee2010, CC BY-SA 4.0. Image details and licence ↗
HOUSEHOLD BILLS · SOLAR POWER · JOBS

Five countries back a plan to double the number of homes with rooftop solar

WHAT HAPPENED

At COP31, Australia, Bangladesh, Kenya, Nigeria and Vanuatu backed a voluntary declaration aiming to double the number of homes worldwide with rooftop solar panels. The goal is to grow from about 150 million homes today to at least 300 million by 2030. The declaration is a promise to work toward the goal, not a law that forces every country to take part.

WHY IT MATTERS

Solar panels turn sunlight into electricity close to where people use it. A home that generates some of its own power may buy less electricity from the grid and could lower its bills over time. Solar can also help remote communities or places hit by disasters, especially when panels are paired with batteries that store power for later.

THE HARD PART

Panels and batteries can be expensive to buy at the start. Homes need suitable roofs, safe wiring and trained installers. Electricity grids may also need upgrades so that power can move safely between homes and the wider system. Without storage, solar panels produce less or no electricity at night and on very cloudy days.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Supporters want governments to create clear rules, offer help with upfront costs and train more installers. They also want better batteries and stronger power lines. The countries backing the declaration hope that more households will be able to produce some of their own electricity instead of depending entirely on distant power stations.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Rooftop solar can give families more control over energy costs and reduce pollution when it replaces fossil-fuel electricity. But the equipment still costs money and needs materials to manufacture. Old panels and batteries must eventually be repaired, reused or recycled. A successful plan must think about the whole life of the equipment, not just the day it is installed.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

If your school installed solar panels, what would you want to know before agreeing: the cost, how much electricity they make, what happens on cloudy days, or how long they last? Which fact would help you decide?

🤖 Technology & Science

One research team is using AI to help check software with mathematics. Another has solved a mystery about a star that keeps flashing.

AI · DIGITAL PRIVACY · CYBERSECURITY

AI agents help researchers mathematically check part of a messaging system's security

WHAT HAPPENED

On November 10, Google DeepMind described a new research system called AlphaProof Code. Human experts worked with AI agents to check important security properties of the code behind the Signal Protocol, a technology used to protect private messages in services including Signal and WhatsApp. The team used a computer language called Lean to check the mathematical proofs produced by the agents.

WHY IT MATTERS

Encryption scrambles messages so that people who are not meant to read them cannot easily understand them. It helps protect conversations, photos and other private information. Checking that the software really follows its security rules is difficult, so a tool that helps researchers find mistakes could be useful for messaging, banking and other important systems.

THE HARD PART

Software can contain millions of tiny instructions, and a weakness may hide in the difference between what a design promises and what the finished code actually does. AI agents can make mistakes too. A mathematical proof only answers the question that researchers carefully wrote down; it cannot prove that every possible risk has been considered.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Researchers first worked with AI to write precise rules describing what the software should protect. Human experts reviewed those rules. AI agents then generated millions of lines of proof code, and Lean checked whether the proofs followed the approved rules. The team also put two old, already-fixed bugs back into an isolated test version, and the system identified that those versions were insecure.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

AI may help experts check more software in less time. But this project did not verify the entire Signal mobile app, every device or every possible attack. WIRED describes it as an important milestone, not proof that all messaging software is now unbreakable. Human judgment and independent testing still matter.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

If an AI system said it had checked a private messaging app, what evidence would you want to see before trusting that claim? Who should decide which safety rules the AI must prove?

SPACE · STARS · NASA CHANDRA

What keeps making this giant star flash every 113 days?

WHAT HAPPENED

Astronomers using NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory have found a likely explanation for repeated bright flashes from a giant star called AT 2016blu. The star is in a galaxy about 30 million light-years away. A hidden companion circles it every 113 days and pulls material away when the two objects come close.

WHY IT MATTERS

The stolen gas heats up as it moves very quickly around the companion, producing powerful X-rays. The discovery helps scientists understand unusual stars that brighten dramatically but survive instead of exploding like a supernova.

THE HARD PART

The companion is still a mystery. Scientists think it may be a black hole or a neutron star — the very dense remains of a star — but they have not yet confirmed which one it is.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Researchers compared years of observations from ordinary light telescopes with a carefully timed Chandra X-ray observation made during a bright outburst in March 2026. Watching the star over time helped them notice the repeating pattern.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

Why might astronomers need to watch an object for many years instead of taking just one picture? What repeating pattern would you look for?

🎨 Creativity & Inspiration

Three high-school researchers show how simple materials and repeated experiments can lead to an engineering idea.

Illustrative photo of a curved gridshell structure; this is not the students' prototype. Photo: Jeanpei, CC BY-SA 4.0. Image details and licence ↗
UNITED STATES · YOUNG ENGINEERS · DESIGN

Three students turn wooden sticks, hot water and 3D-printed parts into curved structures

WHAT HAPPENED

Madison Lee, Taylor Yeh and Joyce Jia Xu worked with an engineering professor at Hofstra University in New York. They developed a low-cost way to shape curved structures using wooden dowels and 3D-printed plastic connectors. Their research was presented at an international engineering conference in Turin, Italy, and the university highlighted their work on November 5.

WHY IT MATTERS

Buildings often use curved roofs and shells because a carefully designed shape can be strong while using materials efficiently. The students explored a way to start with a flat grid and turn it into a three-dimensional curve, which could help architects test ideas without expensive equipment.

THE HARD PART

The connectors were made from a plastic called PLA. When the models were placed in hot water, the connectors softened enough for the flat grid to change shape. The team had to design the connectors, test the models and revise them repeatedly. A model that looks good is not automatically safe or strong enough to support a real roof.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

The students used hands-on experiments and computer-aided design to improve their models. They were the lead designers and first authors of the research paper. Their work was displayed alongside research from universities around the world, including the University of Tokyo and MIT.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Low-cost materials can make experimentation more accessible and help students learn by doing. But plastic connectors still need to be made, tested and eventually managed responsibly. Before the method could be used in a real building, engineers would need to test strength, weather resistance, fire safety and long-term durability.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

Could you make a flat sheet of paper or a grid of drinking straws bend into a strong curved shape? What would you change if the model collapsed?

🐾 Nature & Planet

A wildlife disease is putting pressure on animals that gather in large breeding colonies far from most people.

Southern elephant seal pups at Gold Harbour, South Georgia. This is a wildlife photograph, not a picture of sick animals. Photo: Anne Dirkse, CC BY-SA 4.0. Image details and licence ↗
SOUTH ATLANTIC · WILDLIFE · BIRD FLU

Bird flu is putting southern elephant seals at greater risk

WHAT HAPPENED

A highly infectious bird-flu virus called H5N1 has spread from birds to several kinds of mammals, including southern elephant seals. South Georgia, a remote sub-Antarctic island in the South Atlantic, has been badly affected. A research study found 47% fewer breeding females at three large colonies in 2024 than in 2022. When researchers estimated the effect across the island, they calculated that about 53,000 fewer females were present on breeding beaches that season. A report published today says some colonies have also lost more than 90% of newborn pups.

WHY IT MATTERS

Southern elephant seals are huge marine mammals that gather in large groups to breed. Adult females are especially important because they produce the next generation. The International Union for Conservation of Nature moved the species from “Least Concern” to “Vulnerable” in April 2026, meaning it now faces a higher risk of extinction in the wild.

THE HARD PART

South Georgia is remote, and scientists cannot count every animal. The estimate of 53,000 refers to females missing from breeding beaches, not 53,000 animals individually confirmed dead. Some may have died, while others may not have returned to the beaches. Scientists also need good starting population counts to know how large the long-term change really is.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Researchers are monitoring seal colonies and checking other wildlife species that may be affected. BirdLife International is reassessing several seabirds, including great skuas, northern gannets, Peruvian pelicans and Humboldt penguins. Scientists are also looking at other pressures, such as changes in food supply and habitat, so conservation plans do not focus on only one problem.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Monitoring helps scientists spot trouble early, but reaching remote colonies can be difficult and people must avoid disturbing animals or spreading disease. Conservation groups also have limited time and money, so they must decide which species and places need the most urgent attention. The warning is serious, but it is not proof that southern elephant seals are certain to disappear.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

How could scientists tell whether a seal population is recovering if the animals live on a remote island and cannot all be counted? What tools might help them monitor wildlife without disturbing it?

A Wish in the Dark

By Christina Soontornvat · Ages 8–12 · Fantasy adventure · Newbery Honor Book

In a Thai-inspired city called Chattana, all the lights are controlled by one powerful leader. Pong, a boy who dreams of freedom, escapes from prison. Nok, the prison warden's daughter, sets out to find him — but she begins to question what she has always been told about fairness and justice.

This fast-moving story asks a big question: what makes a community fair? It is a good choice for readers who enjoy adventure, mystery and characters who learn to see the world from someone else's point of view.

Think about it: If you could change one rule in a fictional city to make life fairer, what would you change — and who might disagree?
