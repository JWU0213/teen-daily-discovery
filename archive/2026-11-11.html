CENTRAL AFRICA · PUBLIC HEALTH · SCIENCE

Congo's Ebola outbreak passes 11,000 cases as vaccine research moves forward

WHAT HAPPENED

The Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) in Central Africa has recorded 11,042 Ebola infections and 5,387 deaths, according to the latest tally reported by AP on November 11. WHO says cases have been recorded in five countries. Kenya discharged its final patient today and began a 42-day countdown; the DRC still has cases in eight provinces.

WHY IT MATTERS

The outbreak is caused by Bundibugyo ebolavirus. It spreads through direct contact with body fluids of someone who is sick or has died, or with contaminated objects. People need safe care, clear information and trusted local health workers—not blame or rumours.

THE HARD PART

Conflict, poor roads and crowded clinics make it difficult to reach patients. Fear and misinformation can also reduce trust, while health workers need protective equipment and safe places to treat people.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Teams trace contacts, support clinics and talk with communities. On November 4, WHO recommended two vaccine candidates for a Bundibugyo trial; they still need national approval. Results are expected in early 2027.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

There is no approved vaccine or specific treatment for this Ebola type yet. Trials take time, and vaccines alone cannot stop an outbreak without trained staff, trusted information and safe care.

TÜRKIYE · CLIMATE · INTERNATIONAL COOPERATION

World leaders meet at COP31: promises now need practical plans

WHAT HAPPENED

COP31 runs November 9–20 in Antalya, Türkiye, a Mediterranean city in a country linking Europe and Asia. Nearly 200 countries are negotiating; Australia leads formal talks while Türkiye hosts. The UN chief says progress is too slow.

WHY IT MATTERS

Climate change affects heat, storms, food, water and homes. Using electricity in buses, buildings and factories can reduce oil and gas use—but only if the electricity is cleaner.

THE HARD PART

Countries need reliable, affordable power but have different resources and budgets. A proposed target to raise electricity's share of global energy use to 35% by 2035 is voluntary and does not require that the extra electricity be clean.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Negotiators are discussing clean-energy investment, stronger grids and climate adaptation. Countries are also announcing projects such as rooftop solar, but the key question is how they will be funded and built.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

More electricity can help, but power from coal may still cause pollution. New grids and batteries cost money; a fair transition must also consider workers and household bills.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

What should a climate promise include: cleaner power, affordable bills, reliable supply—or all three?

HOUSEHOLD BILLS · SOLAR POWER · JOBS

Five countries back a plan to double the number of homes with rooftop solar

WHAT HAPPENED

Australia, Bangladesh, Kenya, Nigeria and Vanuatu backed a voluntary plan at COP31 to double the number of homes with rooftop solar—from about 150 million to 300 million by 2030.

WHY IT MATTERS

Panels make electricity where people use it. Families may buy less power from the grid, while batteries can store energy for night-time or emergencies.

THE HARD PART

Panels and batteries cost money upfront. Homes need suitable roofs, safe wiring and trained installers; grids may need upgrades to handle power flowing both ways.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Supporters want clear rules, help with upfront costs, installer training and better batteries. The declaration is a goal, not a law forcing every country to join.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Solar can lower bills and pollution, but panels and batteries require materials and eventually need repair or recycling. Savings depend on local sunlight, prices and system costs.

🤖 Technology & Science

One research team is using AI to help check software with mathematics. Another has solved a mystery about a star that keeps flashing.

AI · DIGITAL PRIVACY · CYBERSECURITY

AI agents help researchers mathematically check part of a messaging system's security

WHAT HAPPENED

Google DeepMind says its AlphaProof Code research system worked with human experts and AI agents to prove selected security properties of libsignal, code used by the Signal Protocol. A Lean proof checker checked the mathematics.

WHY IT MATTERS

Encryption scrambles messages so outsiders cannot easily read them. Mathematical checks may help researchers find mistakes in software that protects private conversations.

THE HARD PART

Software has millions of instructions. AI can make mistakes, and a proof only covers the exact rules researchers wrote; it cannot prove they asked every important question.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Humans wrote and reviewed the rules. AI agents generated millions of lines of proof code, and Lean checked them. The team also caught two old bugs when testing them in an isolated version.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

The work covered selected properties of the reference code, not the whole Signal app or group messaging. It is a useful research step, not proof that any app is unbreakable.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

What evidence would you want before trusting an AI system to check software that protects your private messages?

SPACE · STARS · NASA CHANDRA

What keeps making this giant star flash every 113 days?

WHAT HAPPENED

NASA's Chandra telescope helped explain repeated flashes from AT 2016blu, a giant star in galaxy NGC 4559. A hidden companion pulls gas from it every 113 days.

WHY IT MATTERS

The gas heats up and shines in X-rays, helping scientists understand stars that flare dramatically but survive instead of exploding.

THE HARD PART

The companion could be a black hole or a neutron star. Astronomers have not confirmed which.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Researchers combined 15 years of visible-light observations with a Chandra X-ray observation made during an outburst in March 2026.

🎨 Creativity & Inspiration

Three high-school researchers show how simple materials and repeated experiments can lead to an engineering idea.

Illustrative photo of a curved gridshell structure; this is not the students' prototype. Photo: Jeanpei, CC BY-SA 4.0. Image details and licence ↗
UNITED STATES · YOUNG ENGINEERS · DESIGN

Three students turn wooden sticks, hot water and 3D-printed parts into curved structures

WHAT HAPPENED

Three high-school students—Madison Lee, Taylor Yeh and Joyce Jia Xu—worked with Hofstra University researchers to make curved structures from wood dowels, 3D-printed connectors and hot water. Their work was presented at an engineering conference in Turin, Italy.

WHY IT MATTERS

Curved gridshells can be strong while using materials efficiently. A low-cost model lets students and architects test shapes before building anything large.

THE HARD PART

Hot water temporarily softens the PLA plastic connectors, so the team had to control how the flat grid bent. A small model is not proof a real roof is safe.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

The students designed connectors, used computer-aided design and revised their prototypes after repeated tests. They were the lead designers and first authors of the research paper.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Cheap materials make experiments more accessible, but plastic still needs responsible handling. Real buildings would need tests for strength, fire safety and weather.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

Could you bend paper strips or drinking straws into a strong curved shape? What would you change if it collapsed?

🐾 Nature & Planet

A wildlife disease is putting pressure on animals that gather in large breeding colonies far from most people.

Southern elephant seal pups at Gold Harbour, South Georgia. This is a wildlife photograph, not a picture of sick animals. Photo: Anne Dirkse, CC BY-SA 4.0. Image details and licence ↗
SOUTH ATLANTIC · WILDLIFE · BIRD FLU

Bird flu is putting southern elephant seals at greater risk

WHAT HAPPENED

H5N1 bird flu has spread to four of the five main southern elephant seal populations. The Guardian reports more than 53,000 female seals died on South Georgia by 2025; some colonies lost over 90% of newborn pups. A study found 47% fewer breeding females at three colonies in 2024 than in 2022.

WHY IT MATTERS

South Georgia is a remote sub-Antarctic island in the South Atlantic. Adult females produce the next generation, so large losses can slow recovery. The IUCN moved the species to Vulnerable in April 2026.

THE HARD PART

Scientists cannot count every seal on a remote island. The 47% figure comes from three colonies, and population estimates carry uncertainty; not every missing animal is individually confirmed dead.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Researchers monitor colonies and reassess other wildlife. BirdLife International is reviewing four bird species affected by H5N1, including great skuas and Humboldt penguins.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Monitoring helps conservation, but field teams must avoid disturbing animals or spreading disease. Resources are limited, so scientists need reliable counts to decide where help matters most.
