From a changing freshwater lake in Southeast Asia to a trade meeting in East Asia.

CAMBODIA · WATER · FOOD · CLIMATE

Cambodia's great lake is running low—and families are feeling it

WHAT HAPPENED

Tonlé Sap is a huge freshwater lake in central Cambodia. An Associated Press report on November 12 describes low water levels, smaller fish catches and worries about farms as the region faces severe drought. In early October, a river gauge near Prek Kdam was more than a metre below its usual level and about 1.5 metres lower than at the same time last year.

WHY IT MATTERS

About 1.5 million people live on or around the lake. Each rainy season, the Mekong River's rising water pushes back through the Tonlé Sap River and makes the lake grow dramatically. This seasonal “flood pulse” carries nutrients into wetlands and creates places for fish to feed and reproduce. Fish are food and income for many families.

THE HARD PART

Forecasters expect a strong El Niño climate pattern to bring hotter, drier weather to parts of Southeast Asia, but local rainfall is never certain. Upstream dams, sand mining and the loss of flooded forests also affect the lake. Scientists say there is no single cause that explains every change.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Researchers and officials are tracking river levels, drought risks and fish populations. Families are adapting too: when fuel is expensive, some fishers row instead of using boat engines. Protecting flooded forests and giving fish safe places to grow are also important parts of long-term conservation.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Dams can provide electricity and help control water, but changing a river's timing can affect fish and wetlands downstream. Protecting the lake can support food and wildlife, while communities still need jobs, energy and reliable water. The challenge is to plan for all of these needs together.

If your town depended on one lake for food, jobs and transport, what would you do first when the water level dropped?

EAST ASIA · TRADE · DIPLOMACY

Taiwan's place at a major Asia-Pacific trade meeting is in the spotlight

WHAT HAPPENED

Leaders are preparing for the APEC summit in Shenzhen, a technology and manufacturing city in southern China, on November 18–19. Taiwan's President Lai Ching-te said on November 12 that Taiwan would take part on an “equal basis” and named Acer founder Stan Shih as its representative. Beijing says Taiwan's participation should follow China's “one-China” position and APEC rules. Taiwan's government rejects Beijing's claim over the island. Taiwan also reported Chinese military aircraft and ships operating around the island on Thursday; the report described a patrol, not a clash.

WHY IT MATTERS

APEC is a forum where 21 member economies discuss trade, investment and cooperation. Canada, China and “Chinese Taipei” (the name used for Taiwan in APEC) are all members. Decisions and relationships in this region can affect jobs, shipping routes and the supply chains behind phones, computers and other products.

THE HARD PART

Taiwan is self-governed and has its own elected leaders. Beijing says the island is part of China and wants reunification; Taiwan's government says its people should decide their future. These different positions make official meetings and even the wording on invitations difficult.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Taiwan has asked for written assurances about its participation and chosen a business leader as its envoy. China says its representatives can attend under the “one-China” principle and APEC's rules. APEC calls its members “economies” because the forum focuses on economic cooperation, not on deciding every political disagreement.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Meetings can keep trade and practical cooperation going even when governments disagree. But arguments about status and security can make trust harder and distract leaders from shared problems such as reliable shipping, technology standards and energy supplies.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

💰 Economy & Business

A fresh growth report shows how technology, services and household costs connect.

Illustrative London skyline, not an economic chart. Photo: QuintusPetillius, CC BY-SA 4.0. Image details and licence ↗
UNITED KINGDOM · GDP · AI · HOUSEHOLD COSTS

UK economy grows 0.7% in the latest quarter, with technology services among the drivers

WHAT HAPPENED

On November 12, the UK's Office for National Statistics estimated that the economy grew by 0.7% from July to September 2026, after growing 0.5% in the previous quarter. Services grew 0.9%, production grew 0.1%, and construction fell 0.7%. Information and communication grew 2.1%, with computer programming and consultancy up 4.4%. The ONS says this industry has been the largest driver of GDP growth across 2026 so far, and notes that many businesses reporting higher turnover are involved in AI and cloud computing. It also says its data cannot measure AI's exact contribution.

WHY IT MATTERS

GDP is a way to estimate the value of goods and services produced in a country. When businesses sell more or provide more services, it can support jobs and tax revenue. But GDP is not the same as everyone's paycheque, and it does not show whether every family feels better off.

THE HARD PART

Some parts of the economy are not growing. Construction shrank in the quarter, retail sales had weakened in September, and many households and businesses were facing high energy bills and borrowing costs. The ONS also warns that early GDP estimates can be revised when more information arrives.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Companies are investing in software, computing and AI tools that may help workers do some tasks faster. The Bank of England has to decide how to bring inflation down without making borrowing so expensive that families and businesses cut back too much.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Technology investment can create new products and make some work more productive. However, the benefits may not reach every worker or town at the same time. Higher interest rates can help slow inflation, but they also make mortgages and business loans more expensive.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

If a country's economy grows because businesses use more AI, what could help workers share in the benefits?

🤖 Technology & Science

A robot has started building something in space that is too large to launch in one piece.

Photo from ASTRA's in-orbit camera showing the robotic assembly system. Credit: Caltech/Momentus. Caltech image and mission details ↗
SPACE · ROBOTICS · ENGINEERING

A Caltech robot has built part of a structure in space—without astronauts

WHAT HAPPENED

On November 5, Caltech and the US research agency DARPA announced that a robot called ASTRA had assembled a new section of a truss structure while orbiting Earth. It picked up tubes and connectors and joined them together. The robot built the third section, or “bay”; it did not finish the entire planned antenna.

WHY IT MATTERS

Rocket fairings—the protective nose cones around spacecraft—limit how wide a telescope mirror or antenna can be when launched. Building large structures in orbit could one day help create bigger space telescopes, communication antennas or solar arrays.

THE HARD PART

Space is a difficult workshop. The robot must handle tiny parts in microgravity, cope with temperature changes and work with very little direct contact from Earth. Caltech says the team had only seven or eight short communication windows a day, each about 15 minutes long, and had to solve unexpected problems.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Engineers sent commands and checked pictures from the payload's cameras. They also built a matching test unit on Earth, so they could reproduce a problem in the lab, try a fix and then send the instructions to the robot in orbit.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Robots could build structures that are too large or awkward for astronauts to assemble by hand. But this is still a proof-of-concept, not a finished working telescope or antenna. Space hardware is costly, and a mistake can be difficult to repair.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

If a robot notices that one connector is not locked properly, what should it do before adding the next piece?

🎨 Creativity & Inspiration

Four students in India turned classroom electronics into two working prototypes.

Illustrative Bluetooth-controlled Arduino car, not the students' actual project. Photo: shreyansh232, CC BY-SA 4.0. Image details and licence ↗
INDIA · STUDENT INVENTORS · SCHOOL TECHNOLOGY

Four students in Amritsar build a Wi-Fi robot car and a smart attendance prototype

WHAT HAPPENED

Four students at Government Senior Secondary School in Bhangali, near Amritsar in India's Punjab state, built two technology projects using classroom resources. Harmandeep Singh and Manjot Preet Singh made a small car that can be steered with a phone over Wi-Fi. Gurnaz Kaur and Kushpreet Singh made a camera-based attendance system that records a name and time in a spreadsheet. Their projects are scheduled to be shown at a district technology fair on November 18.

WHY IT MATTERS

Engineering is not only about expensive laboratories. A small motor, a battery and a simple computer board can help students learn how circuits, coding and machines work. The students started with problems they could see in their own school.

THE HARD PART

A robot car must respond reliably and stop safely. A face-recognition system has a different challenge: it can make mistakes, and face images are personal information. The report says the system's accuracy and reliability have not been independently verified.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Teachers are helping the students improve their models before the fair. The car's makers have discussed possible future additions such as obstacle sensors, but those are ideas for later—not features confirmed in the current model.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Automatic attendance could save teachers time, and the car is a fun way to learn coding. But a school should ask who can see stored face data, how long it is kept, how mistakes are corrected and whether students have another way to register attendance. A simple ID card or QR code may solve the problem with less personal information.

🐾 Nature & Planet

A surprise discovery off Quebec adds one more calf to a hopeful—but still fragile—season.

Illustrative North Atlantic right whale mother and calf; this is not the newly identified pair near Anticosti Island. Photo: Lisa Conger and Elizabeth Josephson/NOAA Fisheries, public domain. Image details ↗
QUEBEC · OCEAN LIFE · CONSERVATION

A surprise whale calf spotted near Anticosti Island brings good news for a rare species

WHAT HAPPENED

Researchers photographed a North Atlantic right whale mother and calf near Anticosti Island, Quebec, on September 25. After comparing the pictures with years of whale records, scientists identified the mother as Silt, a whale at least 38 years old. The New England Aquarium announced on November 2 that this was her sixth known calf—and the 24th calf counted for the 2025–2026 season.

WHY IT MATTERS

The regular calving season produced 23 mother-calf pairs, the fourth-highest count on record; the later discovery brings the season's total to 24. That is encouraging for a species with an estimated population of only about 380 whales. Each calf that survives and grows up could help the population recover.

THE HARD PART

One good season does not mean the species is safe. Right whales can become tangled in fishing ropes or be hit by ships. Silt herself has survived several entanglements, and scientists still need to find and identify whales across a very large ocean.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Researchers compare photographs to track individual whales, their calves and injuries. In the Gulf of St. Lawrence, Canada uses seasonal fishing-closure rules when whales are detected, tests fishing gear designed to reduce entanglement, and sets speed limits for larger vessels to lower the risk of collisions.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Slower ships and temporary fishing closures can protect whales, but they may also cost shipping companies and fishers time and money. Better gear and warning systems can help people work while reducing risk, but they need testing, training and investment.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

How could coastal communities protect whales while also supporting the people whose jobs depend on fishing and shipping?

The Last Cuentista

By Donna Barba Higuera · Middle-grade science fiction · Newbery Medal and Pura Belpré Award winner

Petra Peña is twelve when a comet threatens Earth. She and her family join a group travelling to a new planet. After a long journey, Petra discovers that she is the only child who still remembers Earth's stories—and those memories may be important for the future.

This adventure mixes space travel with Mexican folklore and a big question: what should people remember when they start again somewhere new? It is thoughtful and sometimes intense, so it is best for readers who enjoy serious science-fiction adventures.

Think about it: If you had to leave home for a new planet, which three stories, songs or traditions would you take with you?

🧠 Three words to keep

Flood pulse

The seasonal rise and fall of a river or lake. In Cambodia, it helps bring nutrients and fish into wetlands.

GDP

Gross domestic product: an estimate of the value of all goods and services produced in a country during a period.

Prototype

An early model of an invention that people build and test before making a finished version.
