MEXICO · EDUCATION · DIGITAL LIFE

Mexico's new school phone rules begin today. What should a fair rule look like?

WHAT HAPPENED

Starting Tuesday, November 3, students in Mexico's primary and secondary schools must keep personal phones and other digital devices unused during the school day, including recess. The rules cover public and private schools in what Mexico calls basic education. Phones can still be brought to school and kept stored. Exceptions include teacher-planned learning activities and emergencies or special needs. High schools and universities have different rules.

WHY IT MATTERS / WHY IT HAPPENED

Mexico's education authorities say the aim is to help students concentrate, talk face-to-face, build friendships and spend less of the school day looking at screens. Think about how often a message, game alert or video can interrupt homework. A school wants students to be able to focus on the lesson and on one another.

THE HARD PART

Not every family sees the change the same way. Some parents told the Associated Press they worry about reaching their children quickly during an emergency, especially in places where families feel safety is a concern. Phones can distract, but they can also help families communicate. That is a real trade-off, not a silly argument.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

The national rules allow planned educational use and emergency exceptions. Schools are being asked to explain the rules to teachers and families. A practical school plan also needs a clear way for a parent to contact the office and for the school to reach families if something unexpected happens.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Possible benefit: fewer interruptions and more time for conversation, games and learning. Possible downside: students may lose a quick way to contact home, and teachers cannot use phones casually as a learning tool. The result will depend on how clearly schools explain exceptions and handle emergencies.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

If you were helping your school write a phone rule, how would you balance concentration, safety, friendship and useful learning technology?

INDIA · WEATHER · COMMUNITY SAFETY

Cyclone Murasu reaches India's east coast: warnings, evacuations and a difficult week

WHAT HAPPENED

Cyclone Murasu made landfall near Ongole in Andhra Pradesh, a state on India's east coast, early on Tuesday, November 3. The India Meteorological Department reported sustained winds of about 100–110 kilometres per hour as the storm crossed the coast. Thousands of people were moved to safer places. Schools closed, and some train services and flights were suspended as heavy rain threatened flooding.

WHY IT MATTERS / WHY IT HAPPENED

Andhra Pradesh faces the Bay of Bengal, a warm part of the Indian Ocean where powerful tropical storms can form. A cyclone brings more than strong wind: heavy rain can flood streets and fields, while waves and rising coastal water can reach low-lying places. Murasu was the second cyclone to affect this area in a week, so many communities were already dealing with damage from the earlier storm, Arnab.

THE HARD PART

Even with forecasts, it is difficult to know exactly where the heaviest rain will fall or how quickly water will rise. Families may have to leave homes, farmers can lose crops, and transport stops can make it harder to get food, medicine or help where it is needed.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Weather officials track the storm and issue warnings. Local authorities organise evacuations, open safer shelters and pause school or transport services when conditions are dangerous. These steps are meant to give people time to move away from exposed coastlines and flood-prone areas.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Good: early warnings and evacuations can save lives. Hard part: closing schools, roads and businesses disrupts daily life, and moving people is stressful. But waiting too long can be much more dangerous. After the storm, communities still need help repairing homes, roads and farms.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

Imagine your family had to leave home for one night because of a storm. What five useful things would you put in a small emergency bag, and how would you make sure everyone knew where to meet?

EUROPE · ENERGY · HOUSEHOLD COSTS

Europe faces a tight gas supply. Germany is paying for backup power.

WHAT HAPPENED

On November 3, Germany awarded contracts worth about €12 billion to build new gas-fired power stations or convert existing ones. Together, they will provide 5.16 gigawatts of power capacity and are expected to be available by 2031 at the latest. The plants are designed to help when wind and solar power are not producing enough electricity. Germany plans to recover the subsidy costs through an extra charge on consumer bills.

WHY IT MATTERS / WHY IT HAPPENED

Europe is entering winter with its lowest natural-gas storage levels in more than a decade, according to a new report from the International Energy Agency (IEA). The IEA says gas shipments through the Strait of Hormuz have been heavily disrupted, reducing supplies from Qatar and the United Arab Emirates. The Strait is a narrow sea route between Iran and Oman. When fewer ships can deliver fuel, countries compete for other supplies.

THE HARD PART

Gas can provide electricity when the weather is not sunny or windy, but new plants cost a lot to build and burning gas releases carbon dioxide. The IEA also says global liquefied natural gas (LNG) supply is expected to fall by about 3% in 2026. A tight market can push prices up for countries that need to buy gas.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Germany is combining more renewable power with gas plants kept ready for times when other sources are not enough. The IEA says more flexible LNG contracts and new supply, including from North America, are helping some countries handle disruptions. Governments are also trying to improve energy efficiency and prepare for winter demand.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Benefit: backup plants can help keep electricity flowing during periods of low wind and sunlight. Cost: the system is expensive, part of the cost may appear on household bills, and gas is still a fossil fuel. The challenge is keeping homes warm and factories running without locking in pollution for too long.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

If you were planning a country's energy system, how would you balance reliable electricity, affordable bills and lower pollution? Would you invest in batteries, more wind and solar, backup power, or a mix?

🤖 Technology & Science

SPACE · GALAXIES · COSMIC MYSTERIES

Hubble finds a surprising oxygen mystery in 37 small galaxies

WHAT HAPPENED

In a study published this week, astronomers used NASA's Hubble Space Telescope and other observatories to examine 37 nearby dwarf galaxies. A dwarf galaxy is a smaller galaxy with far fewer stars than a giant one like the Milky Way. The researchers found that these galaxies contain much less oxygen than computer models predicted.

WHY IT MATTERS / WHY IT HAPPENED

Stars make oxygen and many other elements. When very massive stars explode as supernovas, powerful winds can push gas out of their galaxies. That means some of the oxygen may have travelled into the space around a galaxy. Oxygen is important for understanding how galaxies change over time — and it is also one of the elements that makes life on Earth possible.

THE HARD PART

The oxygen is not simply gone from the universe. Scientists still do not know exactly where most of it ended up. Some may be in a huge, faint cloud around each galaxy; some may have travelled even farther. That gas is difficult to detect because it is spread out and very faint.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

The team combined Hubble images with older observations from NASA's Spitzer Space Telescope and radio telescopes. Researchers hope to use the newer Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope to find more tiny galaxies, then study them with Hubble and Webb to trace where their elements went.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Good: the discovery gives scientists a better clue about how stars recycle elements through space. Hard part: the finding also shows that current computer models are missing something. A good scientific discovery does not always provide an instant answer; sometimes it reveals a better question.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

If you could build a space telescope to find the missing oxygen, what would it need to measure — light, heat, radio waves or something else? Why?

🎨 Creativity & Inspiration

Two middle-school inventors show how a useful idea can begin with something ordinary.

Wileidy Enid Feliciano-Rivera at the 2026 Junior Innovators Challenge: Lisa Fryklund Photography / Licensed by Society for Science · official photo and project details.
PUERTO RICO · INVENTION · LESS WASTE

A school cafeteria peel becomes a possible solution for farmers

WHAT HAPPENED

Thirteen-year-old Wileidy Enid Feliciano-Rivera, from Peñuelas in Puerto Rico, won an invention award in the 2026 Junior Innovators Challenge. She used plantain peels collected from her school cafeteria to make a biodegradable mulch — a material spread over soil to help control weeds.

WHY IT MATTERS

Farmers often use plastic sheets to stop weeds from growing around crops. But plastic can become waste after a growing season. Wileidy tested her plant-based material against plastic mulch and uncovered soil. In her tests, it controlled weeds about as well as the plastic and helped keep the soil cool and moist.

THE HARD PART

A successful school experiment is a starting point, not proof that a product is ready for every farm. The material would need more testing in different soils, weather conditions and crops. It would also need to be affordable and easy for farmers to make or use.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Wileidy's project turns food scraps into a material with a job to do. That is an example of circular thinking: instead of throwing something away, ask whether it can become a useful resource.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Promise: less cafeteria waste and a possible alternative to plastic in farming. Questions: how long does the material last, how quickly does it break down, and can it be made at a useful scale? Those are exactly the kinds of questions that turn an invention into careful science.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

What is one kind of waste at your school — paper, food scraps, packaging or something else — that could become useful? How would you test your idea fairly?

Another clever idea: a tiny fan that samples the air

Bailey Quinn Frank, 15, from Florida, built a low-cost environmental DNA sampler. A small fan pulls air through filter paper, which can collect bacteria for scientists to study. Bailey hopes an affordable tool like this could help rural communities watch for possible health threats. It is a research prototype, not a medical test or a finished public-health product.

🐾 Nature & Planet

Sea-surface temperature map for August 25, 2026: Copernicus Marine Service, CC BY-SA, reproduced with The Conversation's science explainer.
PACIFIC OCEAN · CLIMATE · WEATHER PATTERNS

El Niño is getting stronger — and its weather effects can travel far

WHAT HAPPENED

El Niño is a natural climate pattern in which parts of the tropical Pacific Ocean become warmer than usual and the winds above the ocean change. The US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) says the current El Niño is strengthening and has a greater than 83% chance of remaining strong to very strong through January–March 2027. The World Meteorological Organization expects the warming to peak around December.

WHY IT MATTERS / WHY IT HAPPENED

Normally, steady trade winds push warm surface water westward across the tropical Pacific. During El Niño, those winds weaken and warm water spreads farther east. That changes where air rises, clouds form and rain falls. Some places may become drier; others may face heavier rain. Recent reporting has described drought and fire risks in parts of Indonesia and South America, alongside heavy-rain concerns in other areas.

THE HARD PART

El Niño does not create the same weather everywhere, and it cannot tell forecasters exactly what will happen in one town on one day. Local storms, geography and other climate patterns matter too. Scientists also separate the natural El Niño cycle from human-caused climate change, although the two can combine to make heat and other risks worse.

WHAT PEOPLE ARE TRYING

Forecasters are sharing seasonal outlooks so communities can prepare. Depending on local risks, that may mean storing water, checking drainage, preparing for heat, reducing wildfire danger or helping farmers plan planting times. Early information gives people more time to make decisions.

GOOD AND BAD / TRADE-OFFS

Good: better forecasts can help people prepare before a dry or rainy season. Hard part: forecasts describe chances, not guarantees. A community must prepare for likely risks without assuming that every predicted effect will happen exactly as expected.

🤔 THINK ABOUT IT

If your town had a warning that next season might be unusually hot or dry, what could your school, family or neighbourhood do now to save water and stay safe?

🧠 Three Words to Know

Landfall

The moment a tropical storm or cyclone reaches the coast and moves onto land.

LNG

Short for liquefied natural gas: natural gas cooled until it becomes a liquid, making it easier to ship.

Biodegradable

A material that can break down naturally with help from tiny living things such as bacteria and fungi.
