Global News & Events

Two places, two very different challenges

West Africa · Election watch

Cabo Verde chooses its president again — but many voters stayed home

Imagine ten islands in the Atlantic Ocean, more than 600 kilometres west of Senegal. That is Cabo Verde, also called Cape Verde, a country of about half a million people. Its capital, Praia, sits on the island of Santiago.

What happened

In the November 15 presidential election, incumbent José Maria Neves won a second five-year term, according to provisional results counted from most polling stations. He received about 64% of the vote. His main challenger, Joana Rosa, received about 32% and conceded. Rosa was the first woman in the country’s history to run for president. Final official results are still expected later this week.

Why it matters

The campaign focused on jobs, wages and the cost of living. Many young people leave Cabo Verde to look for work abroad. The prime minister leads the day-to-day government, while the president has important national duties and can veto laws. The election helps decide who will represent the country and influence its direction.

The hard part

Turnout was only about 32% of registered voters. That means roughly two out of every three registered voters did not cast a ballot. Many Cabo Verdeans live overseas, and President Neves said people moving between places and old voter lists may have contributed. That is his explanation; the exact reasons need more study.

What people are trying

Neves has promised to work on higher wages and poverty. He has also called for updated voter registers and suggested electronic voting. Those are proposals, not changes that have already happened. Rosa said her campaign could help open the door to greater equality for women in politics.

Good and bad / trade-offs

The election was competitive, and the challenger accepted the result — both are signs of a peaceful democratic process. But low participation is a warning: leaders need to understand why so many people did not vote. Online voting might help citizens living abroad, but it would need strong security and public trust.

Think about it

If many young adults leave their country to find work, what could a government do to make staying and building a future at home more attractive?

Southeast Asia · International cooperation

A big language-and-culture group presses pause on a leadership disagreement

Cambodia hosted the 20th summit of the International Organisation of La Francophonie in Phnom Penh. The organisation brings together 90 countries and governments connected by the French language. Its members include places where French is widely spoken and places where it is one of several official or working languages.

What happened

At the summit, which ended on November 16, members agreed that Rwanda’s Louise Mushikiwabo would remain secretary-general for one more year. They will choose a new leader after that. The compromise avoided an immediate split over whether she should receive a third term.

Why it matters

The group says it promotes French, cultural diversity, education, peace, democracy and human rights. Its 90 members include countries across five continents, with an estimated 396 million French speakers worldwide. When members disagree, it can become harder to work together on education, culture and other shared projects.

The hard part

The Democratic Republic of the Congo opposed another term for Mushikiwabo and threatened to leave the organisation. Congo, the United States and United Nations experts have accused Rwanda of supporting armed groups in eastern Congo; Rwanda denies the accusations. The summit also faced criticism from human-rights groups, which urged Cambodia to release people detained for peaceful activism and protect democratic freedoms.

What people are trying

The one-year extension gives members more time to discuss the next leader. France said it welcomed the compromise as a way to preserve unity. Separately, Cambodia and France completed a 14-year restoration of the 11th-century West Mebon temple near Angkor, a project involving more than 100 workers and costing about US$12 million.

Good and bad / trade-offs

A compromise can keep a group working together, but it may only postpone the difficult decision. The organisation says democracy and human rights are important values; critics argue that members must apply those values consistently, including when the host country is being criticised. Both cooperation and honest discussion matter.

Think about it

If 90 countries disagree about who should lead their group, what would make a decision feel fair to both the winners and the countries that disagree?

Economy & Business

Can online trade cross borders more easily?

Southeast Asia · Digital business

Eleven countries sign a new digital-economy agreement

On November 14, all 11 members of ASEAN — the Association of Southeast Asian Nations — signed a Digital Economy Framework Agreement, known as DEFA, in Manila, the capital of the Philippines.

What happened

DEFA sets shared rules for parts of the digital economy, including online trade, electronic payments, business digital identities, data moving between countries, cybersecurity and cooperation on artificial intelligence. ASEAN expects the agreement to enter into force in early 2027.

Why it matters

Imagine a small family business in Malaysia selling handmade bags to a customer in Thailand. If both countries accept compatible digital invoices and payment systems, the shop may spend less time on paperwork and more time serving customers. Online businesses can reach buyers far beyond their own towns.

The hard part

The 11 countries do not all have the same internet access, digital infrastructure or business rules. Sharing information can also create privacy and security risks. Small businesses may need training and reliable technology before they can benefit.

What people are trying

Singapore’s government says early projects could include digital payments, trusted digital identities for businesses and electronic trade documents. The agreement also aims to strengthen cooperation on data protection, online scams and cybersecurity.

Good and bad / trade-offs

Common rules could make cross-border shopping and business faster and less expensive. But more online connections can also mean more opportunities for scams or data theft. Governments will need to protect users while allowing useful information to move safely.

Think about it

If you were buying from a small online shop in another country, what information or safety checks would help you trust the seller?

Technology & Science

A message that takes a day to arrive

Space · NASA mission milestone

Voyager 1 is about to reach one light-day from Earth

What happened

NASA says Voyager 1 is expected to reach a distance of one light-day from Earth on Wednesday, November 18, at about 5:16 a.m. Eastern time. That is roughly 16.1 billion miles, or 25.9 billion kilometres. Launched in 1977, Voyager 1 entered interstellar space in 2012 and is still sending information home.

Why it matters

A light-day is a distance, not a travel time: it is how far light moves in 24 hours. Radio messages travel at the speed of light, so a command takes about a day to reach Voyager 1. A reply takes another day to get back. A simple question and answer can take about 48 hours.

The hard part

The spacecraft has been working for almost 50 years, much longer than its original mission. Its power supply slowly weakens, losing about four watts each year. Engineers have had to switch off some equipment and carefully manage energy so the remaining science instruments can keep working.

What people are trying

NASA engineers recently completed an energy-saving project nicknamed “Big Bang” to help Voyager 1 use its power more efficiently. NASA is also inviting people to mark the milestone on November 17 by flashing the letters V-G-R in Morse code with phones or lights. It is a symbolic celebration: a flashlight beam from Earth will not reach the spacecraft.

Good and bad / trade-offs

Voyager continues to send measurements from a region of space no other working spacecraft has explored. But its power and instruments are limited, and messages take a long time. The one-light-day milestone will not change anything aboard the spacecraft; it helps us understand just how enormous space is.

Think about it

If you could send one question to a spacecraft travelling beyond the Sun’s protective bubble, what would you ask it to measure or discover?

Creativity & Inspiration

Two student ideas that look for hidden danger

United States and Philippines · Design engineering

Can a headset spot a possible concussion — and can concrete sense a crack?

What happened

On November 4, the James Dyson Award named two global student-invention winners. At Harvard University in the United States, Cleo Pontone, Radhika Saran and Sophia DeVito created Rumble Rims, a headset that uses infrared cameras to track eye movements during a short screening test. In the Philippines, structural engineer Aldrei Ong created Sentinel, a concrete spacer block that can detect changes linked to hidden cracks.

Why it matters

A concussion is a brain injury that can be difficult to spot, and symptoms may not appear immediately. Hidden cracks inside a building can also be hard to see. Both ideas try to notice warning signs earlier, when people may need a closer check by a trained professional.

The hard part

Neither invention is a magic answer. Rumble Rims is a screening tool, not a medical diagnosis, and it still needs more testing. Sentinel has been embedded in a building for monitoring, but its inventor says more validation and improved electronics are needed before it can be widely used.

What people are trying

The Rumble Rims team plans to build a more advanced prototype and test it with athletes, coaches, trainers and medical advisers. Ong is improving Sentinel’s electronics and studying how its readings compare with real building damage. Both projects began with a simple question: could an everyday safety check be made faster or easier?

Good and bad / trade-offs

More accessible checks could help schools, sports teams and building engineers spot possible problems. But a device that misses a danger or gives a false warning could cause harm or confusion. For a suspected concussion, CDC guidance is clear: the athlete should be removed from play and assessed by a healthcare professional; a headset cannot clear someone to return to sport.

Think about it

Choose a sport, school activity or place in your neighbourhood. What hidden danger could you help people notice earlier, and what simple object could you redesign to do the job?

Nature & Planet

Rare visitors on California beaches

Pacific Ocean · Wildlife conservation

Olive ridley sea turtle hatchlings make their way back to the ocean

What happened

In September, two olive ridley sea turtle nests were found at Seal Beach and Huntington Beach in Southern California. They were the first confirmed natural sea turtle nests on the U.S. West Coast. Because a tropical storm threatened to flood or wash away the eggs, biologists moved them to the Aquarium of the Pacific. The eggs hatched, and hatchlings were released on their beaches on November 14 and 15. A third nest was documented farther north in Ventura County on November 1.

Why it matters

Olive ridleys usually nest in tropical and warm waters. Scientists do not yet know why these turtles chose Southern California. The Aquarium says weather patterns, an incoming El Niño, a storm, a possible range expansion or even a navigation mistake could be involved. One unusual season is interesting, but it does not prove the turtles have permanently moved north.

The hard part

A hatchling’s journey is dangerous. The Aquarium estimates that only about one in 1,000 olive ridley hatchlings reaches adulthood. The species faces threats such as fishing gear, loss of nesting habitat and ocean pollution. Its legal protection status also differs by population: some are listed as endangered and others as threatened.

What people are trying

Wildlife officials and local partners protected the nests, and Aquarium staff incubated the eggs in sand while carefully monitoring temperature and humidity. The hatchlings were returned to the beaches where the eggs were laid. People who spot a nesting turtle or tracks in the sand are asked to keep their distance and tell a lifeguard or wildlife authority.

Good and bad / trade-offs

Moving the eggs gave them a better chance of surviving the storm, but rescuing nests is a special measure, not something people should try themselves. Scientists still need years of careful observations to learn whether this was a rare event or the beginning of a new nesting pattern.

Think about it

How could a beach town help people enjoy the shore while also giving nesting turtles a quiet, safe place?

As she experiments with friends, she discovers that science is about asking questions, testing ideas and learning from results — and that people need care and support, too.

This thoughtful story includes a parent living with depression, so it deals with some serious feelings as well as friendship, curiosity and hope.

Vocabulary to Know

Three useful words from today’s stories

Provisional

Not final yet. Election results can be provisional while officials finish checking the count.

Interoperable

Able to work together. Two countries’ payment systems are interoperable if they can safely connect.

Interstellar

The space between stars. Voyager 1 is travelling through interstellar space beyond the Sun’s protective bubble.
